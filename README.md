## Assessment of Missingness
Starting off this section, there are only three columns of the dataset that have missing values, with them being `name`, `description`, and `rating`. With these columns, I do believe that one of these is MNAR, as in it is missing because of its value, namely `name` (haha get it). This is because there is only one name missing in the entire dataset, which makes me wonder how only one was missing if there was an issue. I believe that this name was missing due to its own value, and some types of columns we could add to turn this from MNAR to MAR, could be the language of the name, and the length of the name, both of which could be reasons as to why the name was not saved.

Now lets check if the `rating` column is MAR on the year the recipe was published. Performing a permutation test on the columns by shuffling `recent_submission` 1000 times. The null hypothesis is that there is no difference in the missingness of `rating` whether or not it was a recent submission, with the alternative hypothesis being that there is a difference in the missingness of `rating` whether or not it was a recent submission at the 0.05 significance level. Here we can see that the results are statistically significant with p-value being essentially zero, meaning that we have sufficient evidence to reject the null hypothesis, that `recent_submission` does not affect `rating`. ## Hypothesis Testing
Now lets try to answer my original question, is cooking worth the time? To do this, I will be doing a permutation test at the 0.05 significance level. The null hypothesis is that recipes under an hour have the same average rating as recipes over an hour. The alternative hypothesis is that recipes under an hour have a higher mean rating than those over an hour. This makes sense to answer my question because it challenges my understanding that to cook anything that tastes good enough to warrant the effort, I need to spend a lot of time, time I don't necessarily have. I used the `rating` and `under_hour` columns and shuffled `under_hour` with the test statistic of a one sided mean diff, that is, the mean rating of under an hour minus the mean rating of over an hour. The permutation test makes sense because we are checking the difference between two distributions, and the test statistic is valid, as we need a one-sided statistic for this one sided hypothesis testing. We care that the hypothesis is one-sided because we want to see if shorter cook time recipes are better than longer ones. The resultant p-value was equal to 0.002, giving sufficient evidence to reject the null hypothesis, that mean ratings for under an hour recipes are the same as the mean ratings for over an hour recipes. Despite this having statistical significance, the observed difference in the dataset was only about 0.015, making the difference almost negligible. This means that, even though recipes under an hour have a higher average rating in this dataset, and evidence has been found to reject the null hypothesis, the difference is so small that its negligible. For my practical life, this means that cooking for less time does not mean that the recipe will taste that much better or worse, freeing me from one of the reasons why I thought that cooking would not be worth it.
## Framing a Prediction Problem
Alright. So I've decided that I might as well cook at home during college. Whats next? Well, lets see if I can figure out the calorie count of these recipes using the nutrition information. This could be useful for me as I can efficiently calculate calories for any ideal macros I may have in mind. Since calorie count is quantitative and continuous, this is a regression problem. The response variable I am predicting is the `calories` column that I created in the data cleaning part of the project. Since we have decided that this is a regression problem, I will be using the coefficient of determination (r^2) to evaluate my model, instead of something like RMSE, because I care more about how much of the underlying relationship we are capturing. There is no timing of prediction issue here, as the macronutrients were measured at the same time as the calories in order to submit the recipe, and is not an issue.
## Baseline Model
For my baseline model, I used a Linear Regression model on the raw values of the `total_fat_pdv` and the `sugar_pdv`, where pdv stands for percentage of daily value. I used these columns because from what I know, low calorie foods are often low fat, and all the media I have consumed has taught me that fitness junkies hate sugar. Both these columns are quantitative, and I did a train-test split on the dataset with a fixed seed to measure the generalizability of my model. When predicting the `calories` column, the r^2 value for train was 0.9006819722587114 and the r^2 value for test was 0.8944663310918366. Based on how little the r^2 value dropped from train to test, I would say that this current model is a good model.
## Final Model
For my final model, I pivoted from a regular linear regression, to a Lasso regression, as I believed that with all the features I am feeding in my final model, the Lasso effectively handles collinearity using its hyperparameter, alpha. The two new features I add here is `carbohydrates_pdv` and `protein_pdv` because from my prior knowledge, I know that athletes always carb load before competitions for energy, and people who work out a lot obsess over their protein intake, and I felt that there was still some information regarding the relationship between nutrition and calorie count missing from my model. As such I believe that these have a large part to play as well in predicting calorie count. But I don't use these features as is. I first standardize total fat, sugar, and carbohydrates to prevent Lasso regression from wrongfully penalizing these as each of these inputs may have different variance and centers. As for protein, however, I noticed that the distribution was skewed, so I log transformed the `protein_pdv` column before using it for it to be a better estimator. I then optimized the hyperparameter by performing gridsearch and using kfold validation of 5. The optimal alpha hyperparameter ended up being 2.44205309454865. The final train-test performance of my model was as follows: r^2 train of 0.9793856057556081 and r^2 test of 0.9771123662759897. My model had an increase in r^2 value from the baseline model of approximately 0.07, and the test vs train difference is quite small, approximately 0.002, leading me to believe that my model now captures more of the relationship between calories and macros as well as it is much better at generalizing.
## Fairness Analysis
Finally, I conducted a fairness analysis using my final model. For this, I was curious if being a baked good meant that my model would have a different accuracy than not being a baked good. To test this, I used Group X as baked goods, Group Y as non baked goods, and the evaluation metric as root mean squared error (RMSE). The null hypothesis for this is that there is no difference in the RMSE of my model predicting calories on baked goods and the RMSE of my model predicting calories on non baked goods. The alternative hypothesis is that there is a difference in the RMSE of my model predicting calories on baked goods compared to non baked goods. This two sided hypothesis is best in this situation as I do not know which direction the difference could be. The significance level I used here is 0.05, and the test statistic is the absolute difference between the RMSE of calories predicted of the `is_baked_good` == True - RMSE of the calories predicted of the `is_baked_good` == False. In order to perform this permutation test, I only used the test split of my data so that the model is not trained on the data it is being evaluated on. The resulting p-value was 0.012, giving me sufficient evidence to reject the null hypothesis. I reject the hypothesis that there is no difference between the RMSE of my model predicting calories of baked goods compared to non baked goods, with sufficient evidence for the alternative. As such, I believe that in this aspect, despite the observed test_statistic being only approximately 64.5 calories, my model is not fair for these two groups.
